# Growth for a crypto trading platform

## The north star, and the metrics beneath it

For an exchange the honest north star is **weekly trading users with positive
retained balance**, not signups, not volume. Volume is dominated by a small number
of large traders and hides a collapsing base. Signups are dominated by whatever
campaign ran last month.

Beneath it, four metrics that actually move:

1. **Activation rate**: signups that reach first trade within 7 days.
2. **Week-2 retention**: activated users who trade again in the second week.
3. **Deposit conversion**: verified users who fund within 14 days.
4. **Reactivation yield**: dormant users who return and stay for 4 weeks, measured
   against a holdout.

## Growth loops that work here

* **Referral with a real reason.** Fee discounts for both sides work better than
  one-sided bonuses, because they survive scrutiny. Referral spikes in bull
  markets and dies in bear markets; do not read the seasonality as programme
  performance.
* **Education as acquisition.** Content that teaches genuinely useful things
  brings in users who trade for years. Content that teaches "how to 10x" brings
  in users who churn in a month and complain.
* **Product-led graduation.** Spot to futures, futures to more products. Each
  graduation increases both revenue and risk, so gate it on demonstrated
  competence rather than on eagerness.
* **The habit loop.** Watchlist, price alerts, and portfolio view are the three
  features that correlate most with retention, because they create a reason to
  open the app when nothing is happening. Drive feature adoption before you drive
  trade frequency.

## Where CRM actually helps, and where it does not

CRM helps when:
* There is a specific action the user is one step away from taking.
* The user has demonstrated intent and stalled.
* Something changed that they would genuinely want to know.
* A habit is forming and needs one more repetition.

CRM does not help when:
* The product experience is the blocker. No push fixes a failed deposit flow.
* The user's reason for leaving was a loss. Messaging accelerates the exit.
* The market is dead. Volume is a market function first and a marketing function
  a distant second. In a flat market, the correct growth strategy is education,
  product depth and cost, not more sends.

Being clear about the second list is what separates a CRM programme that
compounds from one that burns the list.

## Diagnosing a flat number

Work in this order, because the answer is usually near the top:

1. **Market regime.** Compare against the same period's market conditions. Half
   of all "our retention dropped" findings are the market.
2. **Reachability.** Push opt-in, email deliverability, WhatsApp quality rating.
   A silent drop in deliverable audience looks exactly like a drop in interest.
3. **A product change.** Check release dates against the metric break.
4. **Competitor action.** Fees, listings, a promotion.
5. **Campaign fatigue.** Rising sends with falling engagement per send.
6. Only then, creative and targeting.

## The economics of a message

Every send has three costs: the channel cost, the attention cost, and the
opt-out risk. The third one dominates. A push that converts 0.8% and disables
notifications for 0.05% of recipients has spent 0.05% of your future reach
permanently. Over a year of daily sends, that is most of your list.

This is why frequency capping, suppression rules and stage-aware cadence are
growth levers rather than compliance chores.

## Experiment discipline

* One variable per test. Copy or timing or audience, not all three.
* Pre-register the metric and the window before launch.
* Hold out 5 to 10% on anything you plan to repeat.
* Kill campaigns that beat their control by less than the noise. A campaign that
  "works a bit" and runs weekly is a slow tax on your list.
* Keep a written record of the losers. Most teams only remember the winners, and
  re-run the losers every eighteen months.
